\section{Digital Transformation in Higher Education Administration}

The transition from traditional administrative processes to digital ecosystems is a critical focus for modern educational institutions. Early Management Information Systems (MIS) successfully digitized basic student records but frequently functioned as siloed databases that failed to integrate cross-departmental workflows \cite{ref3,ref5}. This fragmentation resulted in underutilized resources, compromised administrative efficiency, and unequal educational access --- not merely because paper was replaced by screens, but because the underlying workflows remained disconnected. Institutions globally have recognized that streamlining student services requires unified digital workflows as a structural prerequisite for operational sustainability \cite{ref46}. Contemporary digital transformation therefore requires interoperable architectures --- systems capable of unifying data collection, processing, and reporting across departments into a single, coherent workflow \cite{ref36}. Kayanja et al. \cite{ref46} confirm in their 2025 study of fully automated and paperless systems in higher education that the most significant efficiency gains emerge not from digitizing isolated records, but from architecturally integrating those records into cross-departmental process flows. This finding is directly applicable to scholarship administration, where eligibility data, document submissions, and deadline tracking currently exist as separate, disconnected processes at most Philippine higher education institutions. The literature therefore establishes that the effectiveness of digital transformation in higher education depends on the adoption of interoperable, cross-departmental architectures --- the foundational design principle motivating ScholarPath AdDU's centralized, Supabase-backed infrastructure.

\section{Web-Based Scholarship Management Systems}

While web technologies are increasingly leveraged for scholarship management globally, the literature focusing on Southeast Asian and Philippine higher education contexts reveals both what existing systems achieve and where they fall critically short. Understanding what these systems implement is necessary before identifying the gap that ScholarPath AdDU is designed to fill. Bagaporo and Espinosa \cite{ref12} developed an Online Scholarship Management System for Camarines Sur Polytechnic Colleges (CSPC), implementing a centralized digital repository that consolidates scholarship listings, automates announcement dissemination to enrolled students, and provides an administrative dashboard for monitoring scholarship status. The system successfully eliminated the need for physical bulletin boards and improved information reach across the multi-campus institution. However, the platform stops at information dissemination: document submission remained a manual, offline process, and there was no mechanism for automated eligibility matching. Students who discovered a scholarship through the portal were still required to process physical paperwork and submit to the respective offices in person \cite{ref12}. Daluyon and Bilog \cite{ref14} developed a Scholarship Management Information System specifically for State Universities and Colleges (SUCs) in Rizal, incorporating online application submission and a structured administrative review interface. Their system advanced beyond the CSPC implementation by digitizing the submission process, reducing paper routing, and enabling administrators to view and sort applications from a centralized dashboard. Crucially, however, Bilog's evaluation found that the absence of automated eligibility validation was the primary driver of application errors and administrative rework across multi-campus deployments. Students continued to self-assess their eligibility and frequently submitted applications for grants they did not qualify for, generating review bottlenecks for administrative staff \cite{ref14}. This finding directly motivates the automated Exclusion Flag Hierarchy in ScholarPath AdDU's Smart Eligibility Checker. Al-Ayyubi and Maulana \cite{ref3} designed a web-based scholarship management information system for a higher education institution in Indonesia, achieving efficient dissemination of scholarship information and basic application form routing through a unified web interface. The system demonstrated measurable improvements in processing speed and information accessibility. Yet the researchers themselves identified the architectural absence of integrated, secure document handling as a critical and recurring gap --- applicants uploaded details through forms, but prerequisite documents such as transcripts and income proofs were still submitted physically and managed separately \cite{ref3}. This precise gap informs the Document Vault architecture in ScholarPath AdDU, which is designed to decouple document storage from application routing and enable one-time upload, multi-attach reuse. At the international level, Amer \cite{ref5} at the Lebanese American University developed a Smart Scholarship Management System as an all-in-one solution, integrating application submission, document handling, and status tracking into a unified platform. The system demonstrated the technical feasibility of end-to-end digital scholarship administration but was built for a single institution with a small, well-defined portfolio of scholarships and did not address multi-variable eligibility matching across taxonomically diverse grant categories. The localization challenge --- adapting to the intersection of internal endowments, external corporate foundations, and state-sponsored grants under distinct eligibility taxonomies --- was not a design constraint of that system. The reviewed literature therefore reveals a clear progression in capability: from static digital repositories \cite{ref12}, to submission-enabled portals \cite{ref14,ref3}, to integrated multi-function platforms \cite{ref5} --- with none demonstrating a validated, end-to-end solution that simultaneously integrates automated multi-variable eligibility matching, secure document vaulting, and proactive notification within a single platform tailored to a localized university taxonomy. This convergent architectural gap directly motivates the ScholarPath AdDU system.

\section{Data Organization and Automated Matching in Information Systems}

In digital scholarship repositories, the technical mechanism by which students are matched to eligible grants varies substantially across the reviewed literature --- and these differences in mechanism, not merely in feature completeness, determine the quality of matching outcomes. Three dominant implementation patterns emerge: keyword-overlap matching, threshold-based sequential rule evaluation, and machine-learning-driven classification, each carrying distinct computational behaviors and institutional tradeoffs. Keyword-overlap matching operates by tokenizing a student's profile fields and tallying term coincidences against grant descriptions. Systems relying solely on this mechanism are computationally incapable of evaluating intersecting numerical constraints --- QPI thresholds, household income ceilings, degree program restrictions --- as a unified logical gate. Orgianus et al. \cite{ref36} document this explicitly: keyword-based retrieval produced persistent false-positive matches because a keyword engine performs term frequency comparisons, not constraint satisfaction, and cannot evaluate ``QPI $\geq$ 2.75 AND income $\leq$ ₱300,000 AND degree $\in$ \{BS CS, BS IT\}'' as a single pass/fail expression. Threshold-based sequential rule evaluation, as implemented by Bilog \cite{ref14}, advances beyond keyword matching by encoding eligibility criteria as conditional logic --- but evaluates constraints independently and without formal priority ordering, producing conflict resolution failures at edge cases that Section 2.5 examines in detail. A third pattern --- machine-learning classification --- is documented by Kanwal et al. \cite{ref50}, whose C4.5 decision tree achieved 95.62\% prediction accuracy on scholarship eligibility data. However, ML-derived decision boundaries carry two institutional liabilities for a multi-pipeline environment like AdDU: the model requires retraining whenever grant criteria change, and its decisions offer no interpretable audit trail --- a compliance requirement under RA 10173 that the authors themselves acknowledge \cite{ref50}. These findings establish a technical hierarchy of tradeoffs: keyword matching fails at constraint evaluation; sequential rule evaluation fails at conflict resolution; ML classification fails at policy interpretability and update responsiveness. ScholarPath AdDU's Smart Eligibility Checker addresses all three through a forward-chaining rule engine with an Exclusion Flag Hierarchy, the design specifics of which are examined in the context of prior deployed implementations in Section 2.5.

\section{Search and Filtering Mechanisms for Financial Aid Discovery}

To understand why dynamic faceted search represents a technically superior retrieval architecture for scholarship repositories, it is necessary to examine how keyword search operates at a structural level --- not merely what it fails to do, but why it fails given the specific retrieval demands of a multi-pipeline financial aid database. Keyword search systems maintain, at their technical core, an inverted index: a data structure that maps individual terms to the ordered list of records in which they appear (posting lists). When a user submits a query, the engine retrieves the posting lists for each query term and intersects or unions them, ranking results by term frequency or relevance weighting \cite{ref43}. This mechanism performs correctly when query vocabulary aligns with document vocabulary --- but scholarship repositories present a structural retrieval mismatch on both sides of this requirement. On the query side, students are frequently unaware of the precise grant name, sponsor organization, or category keyword: a student searching ``income-based scholarship'' retrieves zero results from a grant catalogued as ``Grant-in-Aid Fund,'' despite the GIA being precisely what that student qualifies for. On the document side, a keyword engine is structurally incapable of filtering by continuous numerical attributes --- it cannot natively express ``show me grants where a QPI of 2.8 satisfies the minimum threshold'' without a separate constraint-evaluation layer, because inverted indexes store term occurrences, not numerical bounds. Mahdi et al. \cite{ref4} confirm this limitation in their review of 47 deployed information retrieval systems, finding that keyword-only retrieval consistently underperforms in repositories where items carry multiple intersecting categorical and numerical attributes. Dynamic faceted search resolves this structurally by inverting the retrieval model: rather than the user constructing a query and the system returning documents, the system exposes its underlying metadata taxonomy as interactive filter controls, allowing users to iteratively reduce a large result set by selecting attribute values --- without requiring prior knowledge of specific grant names or internal classification conventions \cite{ref43}. The query mechanism shifts from posting-list intersection over terms to predicate conjunction over structured metadata fields, a fundamentally different computational operation that directly supports the multi-attribute nature of scholarship eligibility. The performance characteristics of this approach, however, are tightly coupled to the indexing architecture underlying the query executor. For categorical attributes with low cardinality --- such as grant category (four values in ScholarPath AdDU's taxonomy) or active status (binary) --- B-Tree indexes support O(log n) key lookups and range scans that retrieve qualifying rows without full-table sequential scans. For continuous numerical attributes --- specifically QPI requirements and application deadlines --- Range indexes support boundary-constrained retrieval, enabling the query planner to access directly the subset of records satisfying Grant.MinimumQPI $\leq$ student.QPI without scanning disqualified rows \cite{ref36}. When compound indexes are constructed across combinations of these fields --- for example, a composite index on (Grant\_Category, Status, QPI\_Requirement) --- the query planner can satisfy multi-facet filter combinations from a single traversal rather than executing independent scans per facet and joining their results in memory, reducing query execution complexity from O(k $\times$ n) to O(log n) for k simultaneously active filters. Tunkelang \cite{ref43} establishes that the quality of the underlying metadata taxonomy is the decisive determinant of whether this architecture reduces or merely redistributes cognitive load: poorly normalized facets produce overlapping filter categories that reintroduce the ambiguity the search was designed to eliminate. What the reviewed literature does not document is a validated deployment of this compound-indexed faceted search architecture within a Philippine higher education financial aid portal managing the concurrent taxonomic diversity of internal endowments, external corporate foundations, and state-sponsored grants --- the precise multi-source structure of AdDU's 3-pipeline portfolio. This is not a feature gap but an architectural and taxonomic gap: prior implementations either deploy keyword retrieval over a homogeneous grant set, or deploy faceted search over repositories without the compound categorical-and-numerical attribute structure that characterizes AdDU's financial aid taxonomy. ScholarPath AdDU addresses this through B-Tree and Range compound indexes over four primary metadata facets, with client-side input debouncing at 300 milliseconds to prevent redundant query execution during iterative filter selection.

\subsection{Policy Desynchronization in Manual Systems}

A critical vulnerability of decentralized, paper-based administrative systems is policy desynchronization --- the structural mismatch between government fiscal year release cycles and academic year timelines \cite{ref16}, compounded by the multi-campus application error patterns documented by Bilog \cite{ref14}. When external partners update their eligibility criteria or deadlines, localized bulletin boards --- maintained as static HTML pages or physical notices --- have no programmatic mechanism to propagate these changes to dependent application records or notify students of the update. The data model underlying these systems is the root cause: eligibility information and deadline data are stored as unstructured content in page-rendering layers rather than as structured, queryable records in a relational database. A change to a grant's QPI threshold is therefore a content editing operation, not a data update, and carries no guarantee of consistency across all instances where that criterion is referenced. Catibog \cite{ref16} identifies this structural weakness as a primary driver of the discrepancies between student-facing scholarship criteria and the criteria actually applied by administrative offices at award time. Centralized, database-driven platforms are architecturally positioned to resolve this through a single-source-of-truth model --- but validated implementations of such systems that explicitly manage the interplay between internal university processing timelines and unpredictable external portal schedules remain absent from the reviewed Philippine HEI literature.

\section{Rule-Based Matching and Faceted Search in Deployed Systems}

While Section 2.3 established the theoretical case for automated eligibility matching and Section 2.4 identified dynamic faceted search as the leading discovery mechanism, it is necessary to examine how these technologies have been implemented in actual deployed systems. This examination provides the empirical grounding for the algorithmic choices made in ScholarPath AdDU and distinguishes the present study's implementation from prior work. Rule-based eligibility matching has been deployed in several educational information systems, with varying degrees of constraint complexity. In the Philippine context, Bilog \cite{ref14} implemented a rule-based validation layer within the Scholarship Management Information System for SUCs in Rizal, where eligibility constraints --- including GPA thresholds, institutional affiliation, and enrollment status --- were encoded as conditional logic applied during the application intake process. While this implementation successfully reduced erroneous submissions at the point of entry, it evaluated constraints sequentially and independently, without a mechanism for resolving conflicts when multiple rules applied simultaneously. This limitation --- the absence of a priority-based conflict resolution strategy --- resulted in edge cases where students holding concurrent government grants passed initial validation for additional state-sponsored awards \cite{ref14}. ScholarPath AdDU addresses this directly through the Exclusion Flag Hierarchy, a priority-based conflict resolution mechanism in which specific negative constraints unconditionally override general positive inclusion rules, a design choice derived from the conflict resolution gap identified in Bilog's implementation. At the international level, Amer \cite{ref5} implemented an eligibility filtering component within the Smart Scholarship Management System at the Lebanese American University that matched student academic profiles against scholarship criteria defined in a centralized database. The system used a multi-criteria filter that evaluated grade point average, program of study, and nationality. However, the filter operated on a flat, non-hierarchical rule set without weighted prioritization, and the scholarship portfolio managed was taxonomically uniform --- all grants originated from the same institutional source, eliminating the policy desynchronization challenge posed by multi-source, externally governed grants \cite{ref5}. ScholarPath AdDU's matching engine operates across four taxonomically distinct grant categories --- internally funded endowments, corporate foundations, state-sponsored grants, and specialized service pipelines --- requiring a more granular constraint architecture than prior implementations demonstrate. Regarding faceted search, Mahdi et al. \cite{ref4} conducted a comprehensive review of dynamic faceted search implementations across information retrieval systems, identifying compound indexing and real-time metadata synchronization as the dominant performance optimization strategies in large-scale deployments. Their analysis of 47 systems confirms that faceted search outperforms keyword-only retrieval particularly in repositories where items carry multiple, intersecting categorical attributes --- precisely the structure of AdDU's scholarship database, where each grant carries attributes across funding origin, coverage type, eligible degree programs, QPI thresholds, and income ceilings \cite{ref4}. Tunkelang \cite{ref43} further establishes that the effectiveness of faceted search is contingent on the quality of the underlying metadata taxonomy: poorly normalized facets produce ambiguous or overlapping filter categories that reintroduce the cognitive burden the search is meant to reduce. This finding directly informed ScholarPath AdDU's backend categorization strategy, in which the 3 financial aid pipelines are normalized into four primary metadata facets with compound B-Tree and Range indexes to ensure unambiguous filter behavior and real-time retrieval without system latency. Taken together, the reviewed implementations establish that rule-based eligibility matching and dynamic faceted search are validated approaches in educational portal contexts, but that no prior system has simultaneously deployed conflict-resolving rule-based matching and compound-indexed faceted search within a single platform managing a taxonomically diverse, multi-source scholarship portfolio. The technical architecture a system adopts for cross-platform delivery determines its responsiveness under variable connectivity, its ability to deliver proactive notifications, and its maintenance overhead --- all of which have direct consequences for adoption rates and operational sustainability. Three dominant architectural patterns exist for cross-platform academic portals, each representing a distinct tradeoff between development complexity, performance characteristics, and native capability access. Responsive web applications (RWAs) adapt a single web codebase to varying viewport sizes through CSS media queries and fluid grid layouts, requiring no separate build for mobile targets but limited to capabilities available through the browser's JavaScript API surface. Progressive Web Applications (PWAs) extend this pattern by registering Service Workers --- background scripts that cache application shells and API responses in the browser's Cache Storage --- enabling partial offline functionality and reducing cold-start load times under intermittent connectivity \cite{ref33}. However, iOS imposes persistent platform restrictions on PWA background execution and push notification delivery through WebKit that reduce their reliability as proactive alert channels; scholarship applications with time-critical deadline notifications cannot depend on this model for consistent delivery. Hybrid mobile architectures --- specifically the Capacitor framework used in ScholarPath AdDU --- resolve this by compiling the same Vite production JavaScript bundle into native Android and iOS packages: Capacitor instantiates a WKWebView (iOS) or WebView (Android) host process and bridges a set of native plugin APIs --- including push notifications via APNs and FCM, local filesystem access, and camera --- into the JavaScript execution context through a typed bridge interface. This architecture delivers the development efficiency of a unified JavaScript codebase while accessing native OS capabilities that browser-based delivery cannot reach, without requiring a parallel Swift or Kotlin codebase. The functional case for this architectural choice is reinforced by documented adoption evidence specific to the Philippine context. The World Bank's \cite{ref48} assessment of digital transformation in Philippine higher education explicitly identifies mobile-first design as a structural prerequisite for adoption --- not a secondary usability consideration --- particularly for Mindanao-based institutions where smartphone access substantially exceeds laptop ownership rates. Yoo and Huang \cite{ref49} further establish that platforms perceived as culturally or technologically misaligned with users' existing digital habits generate adoption failure through reversion: when a portal's interface model conflicts with how a user's primary device (typically a mid-range Android smartphone) renders and interacts with web content, users abandon the digital channel and return to manual processes, recreating precisely the administrative bottlenecks the platform was deployed to eliminate. The reviewed literature therefore establishes both the technical case for a hybrid architecture and the documented adoption consequences of under-engineering the mobile delivery layer. What prior scholarship portal implementations in the Philippine literature have not documented is a formal operationalization of this pattern --- specifically the Capacitor-wrapped production build approach producing a single deployable codebase for web, Android, and iOS --- within a financial aid management context. ScholarPath AdDU's hybrid architecture directly addresses this gap, enabling the automated notification subsystem to deliver time-critical deadline alerts through native push channels that browser-based web portals cannot reliably access.

\section{Usability and User Experience in Educational Platforms}

In evaluating web-based academic systems, usability testing is universally identified as the most vital assessment method. Task-based usability testing directly measures how well users can complete real tasks under realistic conditions. To complement observed performance, researchers rely on standardized perception-based instruments like the System Usability Scale (SUS). Bangor, Kortum, and Miller \cite{ref13} conducted an extensive empirical evaluation of the SUS, confirming its high reliability and validity across diverse digital interfaces. Empirical research by Tullis and Stetson \cite{ref42} further demonstrated that a sample size of 10 to 12 participants is sufficient to yield reliable SUS scores that accurately reflect the broader user experience. When these standardized instruments are used alongside the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Evaluation model \cite{ref31}, developers can effectively quantify perceived usability and ensure the interface minimizes administrative friction. The evaluative methodology of this study is built on this established consensus: task-based usability testing paired with the SUS and the ISO/IEC 25010 quality framework constitutes the field's most validated approach for determining whether an academic platform genuinely reduces the friction it was designed to address \cite{ref13,ref31,ref42}. Applying this protocol to ScholarPath AdDU provides the empirical backbone through which the study's claims about discovery efficiency, eligibility accuracy, and deadline management are substantiated.

\section{Policy, Data Privacy, and Access Control in Financial Aid Information Systems}

Financial aid management systems process data that is simultaneously sensitive, multi-tenanted, and multi-role: a student's household income record must be readable by OSA administrators but invisible to other students; a document's verification status must be writable by OSA staff but read-only to Department Chairs; application records must be queryable by administrators globally but accessible to students only for their own submissions. The technical mechanism by which these constraints are enforced --- not merely the policy that states they should be enforced --- determines whether a system achieves genuine RA 10173 compliance or only nominal compliance. The two dominant access control paradigms in information systems differ fundamentally in their enforcement architecture. Access Control Lists (ACLs) enforce access by maintaining, for each data object, an explicit list of subjects and their permitted operations. In a university scholarship context, this means maintaining per-student permission entries for each application record --- a model that scales as O(users $\times$ objects) and requires manual permission reassignment each time a student's status changes or an administrator's scope is modified. More consequentially, ACL enforcement in web applications is typically implemented at the application layer: a middleware check verifies the requesting user's permission before executing the database query. This model carries a structural security vulnerability: if an API endpoint is inadvertently exposed through a misconfigured route, a CORS policy error, a parameter injection attack, or a client-side logic bypass, the database itself executes the query without resistance --- the application-layer permission check that was bypassed leaves no second line of defense at the data tier \cite{ref38}. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), formalized by Ferraiolo and Kuhn \cite{ref24} and standardized through NIST \cite{ref35}, resolves the scalability problem through role indirection: permissions are attached to roles rather than to individual users, reducing permission management from O(users $\times$ objects) to O(roles $\times$ permissions). The more architecturally significant property of RBAC for RA 10173 compliance, however, is its capacity for enforcement at the database engine level through PostgreSQL's Row Level Security (RLS) mechanism. An RLS policy is a predicate expression --- for example, USING (student\_id = auth.uid()) on the Applications table --- that PostgreSQL evaluates as part of every query's execution plan, appended automatically before any rows are returned to the requesting process. Because this predicate is evaluated within the query executor itself, it operates below the application layer entirely: there is no code path --- whether through a frontend routing error, a misconfigured API proxy, or a direct database connection --- through which a student can access another student's application record, because the filter is enforced by the database engine, not by the application that queries it. Despite this technically superior enforcement model, Halili \cite{ref26} documents that Philippine HEI scholarship portals commonly lack transparent, auditable access control implementations, and CHED's memorandum guidelines \cite{ref18} do not specify the technical mechanism through which access segregation must be achieved --- leaving institutions free to implement application-layer checks that carry the structural vulnerability described above. The consequence is that existing Philippine scholarship portals may be formally RA 10173-compliant at the policy documentation level while remaining technically vulnerable at the implementation level --- a gap that is not visible in policy audits but would be exploitable through standard API testing. ScholarPath AdDU addresses this gap by enforcing its RBAC permission matrix through PostgreSQL RLS policies that bind role-to-permission rules directly to the database engine across all three system roles: undergraduate students (self-access only), OSA administrators (global read/write for application processing), and Department Chairs (read-only, department-scoped). This approach ensures that access segregation is architecturally unavoidable rather than policy-dependent, and that the platform's RA 10173 compliance is verifiable at the database schema level rather than contingent on the correctness of application-layer code.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

This study is anchored on the Information Systems Success Model \cite{ref20}, a widely validated framework that evaluates the effectiveness of centralized web portals. The model posits that the success of an information system is driven by six interrelated dimensions: System Quality, Information Quality, Service Quality, System Use, User Satisfaction, and Net Benefits. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:issm}, this standard model has been explicitly adapted to map the operational variables and institutional constraints of the ScholarPath AdDU platform. \begin{figure}[htbp]

System Quality is represented by the portal's 24/7 accessibility, the precision of its automated matching algorithms, and its secure data architecture --- each of which responds to the system-architecture gaps documented in the reviewed literature \cite{ref3,ref12,ref14}. Information Quality is reflected in the accuracy of the consolidated scholarship details, real-time deadline notifications, and precise QPI tracking --- addressing the policy desynchronization and information fragmentation identified in \cite{ref16} and \cite{ref36}. Service Quality encompasses the system's responsiveness, including clear error messaging for students and prompt workflow support for the Office of Admission and Aid --- informed by the cultural alignment requirements documented in \cite{ref49} and \cite{ref48}. According to the model, high performance in these foundational areas directly drives user engagement. As students and OSA administrators experience reliable system and information quality, their System Use (e.g., uploading prerequisite documents, processing applications) and User Satisfaction (measured quantitatively via the System Usability Scale) will increase. Ultimately, this sustained engagement leads to the system's Net Benefits: eliminating manual administrative bottlenecks, preventing missed scholarship deadlines, and ensuring an equitable, data-driven distribution of financial aid across the university. Crucially, this entire framework operates strictly within the parameters of institutional policy and national data privacy laws, with all dimensions of system success governed by the compliance requirements of RA 10173 through the RBAC architecture established in \cite{ref24,ref38}.
