\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

This chapter details the systematic procedures, processes, and tools utilized in the design, development, and evaluation of the ScholarPath AdDU system. It outlines the research design, system development methodology, system architecture, data gathering instruments, and statistical tools used for data analysis.

\section{Research Design}

The research design incorporates a comparative usability evaluation to validate the system's impact on processing efficiency. During the initial requirements gathering phase, the researchers will administer a pre-study time-on-task survey to a purposive sample of existing scholars. This survey aims to measure the average duration students currently spend discovering financial aid options and routing physical documents (e.g., Form 230-SCH). Once the ScholarPath AdDU prototype is developed, a controlled, task-based usability testing session will be conducted. The perceived time and effort recorded in the Pre-Study Student Problem Validation Survey (Appendix B) will then be compared against the corresponding items in the Post-Prototype Perceived Effort Survey (Appendix C), which is administered to the same participants immediately following the prototype session. Both instruments use identical ordinal and Likert-scaled items, enabling direct item-to-item mean comparisons to determine whether participants perceive the digital workflow to require significantly less time and effort than the manual process they described at baseline. The design and administration of both instruments, and the statistical procedure for their comparison, are detailed in Section 3.4.3. This comparative analysis will quantify the reduction in application processing time and validate the system's perceived performance efficiency. The comparative evaluation follows a within-subjects design, meaning the same cohort of participants who complete the Student Problem Validation Survey (establishing perceived baseline effort) will subsequently participate in the prototype usability testing session. This design is chosen over a between-subjects approach because it eliminates inter-group variability --- each participant serves as their own control, ensuring that any observed difference in perceived effort is attributable to the system rather than individual differences in prior scholarship experience. A sample of 10 to 15 participants is sufficient for this design given that SUS reliability stabilizes at that range \cite{ref42}, and the time-perception comparison uses ordinal scaled data that does not require large-N inferential statistics. This study employs a Developmental Research Design, specifically focusing on System Development. This approach is the most appropriate for IT and Computer Studies capstone projects, as it centers on the structured creation, implementation, and evaluation of a functional software solution to address a specific operational problem. In this context, the design governs the transition of the University Scholarship Office's manual scholarship application process into a centralized, web-based digital ecosystem. The developmental design is complemented by a descriptive evaluation phase to assess the prototype's usability and functional suitability.

\section{System Development Life Cycle (SDLC)}

To ensure flexibility and iterative improvement, the development of ScholarPath AdDU follows the Agile Development Methodology. Unlike rigid, linear models, Agile allows the development team to adapt to evolving requirements from stakeholders --- including University Scholarship Office staff, School Scholarship Subcommittee members, and Office of Admission personnel --- through continuous feedback loops between phases. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sdlc}, the development process is organized into five iterative phases, each building upon the outputs of the last. \begin{figure}[htbp]

  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figure2_sdlc.png}
  \caption{ScholarPath AdDU Agile SDLC Flowchart}
  \label{fig:sdlc}
\end{figure} The first phase involves initial data collection through Key Informant Interviews (KII) conducted with primary stakeholders, including University Scholarship Office staff, the Office of Admission, and the School Scholarship Subcommittees. This phase identifies the system's exact functional requirements, existing manual bottlenecks (e.g., Form 230-SCH handling), and the specific student data attributes necessary for the automated scholarship matching algorithm. Phase 2: System Design. Drawing from the gathered requirements, this phase produces the architectural blueprint of the system. Deliverables include the relational database schema, User Interface/User Experience (UI/UX) wireframes for both student and administrator views, and the logic flow underpinning the Smart Eligibility Checker. Phase 3: Development. The core programming phase translates design artifacts into a functioning system. Front-end interfaces are built using Vite as the primary build tool, paired with a component-based JavaScript framework (React or Vue) for a reactive, modular UI. Back-end infrastructure --- including the PostgreSQL database, secure Document Vault storage, and user authentication --- is managed entirely through Supabase. To extend the application to mobile platforms, Capacitor wraps the Vite production build into native Android and iOS packages, enabling cross-platform deployment without maintaining a separate codebase. Phase 4: Testing. Prior to wider release, the system undergoes alpha testing to verify that core functionalities --- such as document uploading and automated criteria matching --- operate without critical errors. Issues surfaced during this phase are documented and fed back into development for resolution before proceeding. Phase 5: Deployment and Evaluation. The functional prototype is deployed to a purposive sample of end-users for User Acceptance Testing (UAT). System quality is then assessed using standardized usability metrics defined by the ISO/IEC 25010 standard, providing quantitative and qualitative data to inform further refinement. As the feedback loop in Figure~\ref{fig:sdlc} indicates, findings from the evaluation phase are not terminal --- they inform subsequent iterations, allowing requirements, design decisions, and development priorities to be revisited as stakeholder needs become clearer through real-world use.

\section{System Architecture and Algorithmic Logic}

The architectural decisions underlying ScholarPath AdDU are not derived independently --- each major component is a direct response to a documented gap in the reviewed literature. The choice of a centralized Supabase/PostgreSQL backend was motivated by the policy desynchronization documented in Catibog \cite{ref16} and Orgianus et al. \cite{ref36}, where decentralized, multi-campus systems produced deadline mismatches and contradictory eligibility information that a single canonical database is architecturally positioned to eliminate. The Document Vault --- a one-time upload, metadata-tagged, multi-attach storage architecture --- directly addresses the integrated document handling gap identified independently by both Al-Ayyubi and Maulana \cite{ref3} and Bagaporo and Espinosa \cite{ref12}, whose systems demonstrated measurable processing improvements while still requiring physical document submission as a separate, offline step. The Rule-Based Eligibility Engine with its Exclusion Flag Hierarchy was motivated by Bilog's \cite{ref14} finding that student self-assessment of eligibility was the primary driver of application errors in multi-campus deployments, and that sequential rule evaluation without conflict resolution produced edge cases where students with concurrent government grants passed eligibility checks for additional state-sponsored awards. The Dynamic Faceted Search with compound B-Tree and Range indexing was grounded in Mahdi et al.'s \cite{ref4} confirmation that faceted mechanisms outperform keyword retrieval in repositories with multiple intersecting categorical attributes, and Tunkelang's \cite{ref43} finding that the quality of the underlying metadata taxonomy determines whether faceted search reduces or merely redistributes cognitive load. The automated multi-channel notification subsystem responds to the missed-deadline patterns attributable to passive communication channels, documented as a structural weakness in decentralized administration by Catibog \cite{ref16}. Finally, the hybrid JavaScript mobile-and-web architecture via the Capacitor wrapper was directly motivated by the World Bank's \cite{ref48} caution that digitization without mobile-first design fails to achieve adoption in Philippine HEI contexts, particularly for Mindanao-based student populations. These connections are what distinguish ScholarPath AdDU's design as an evidence-grounded architectural response rather than a general-purpose digitization exercise.

\subsection{The ``Smart Eligibility Checker'' (Automated Matching Engine)}

This engine actively evaluates three primary student parameters --- Quality Point Index (QPI), household income, and enrolled degree program --- against the specific constraints of each grant. Student QPI is self-reported during profile registration (see Task 1, Section 3.4.1) and stored in the system's PostgreSQL database. The platform does not integrate with the AdDU University Registrar's database; QPI data accuracy is therefore dependent on the student's own input at the time of registration and is not programmatically verified against official academic records prior to eligibility matching. To handle institutional edge cases and policy contradictions --- such as desynchronized guidelines where a general STEM grant might implicitly exclude BS Nursing due to separate college undergraduate-specific clinical fees --- the engine utilizes an Exclusion Flag Hierarchy. In this logic, specific negative constraints (exclusions) always override general positive constraints (inclusions). Pseudocode for the Matching Algorithm: The following pseudocode illustrates the sequential logic processed by the backend engine when a student requests a scholarship match: \begin{lstlisting}[caption={Pseudocode of the Smart Eligibility Checker matching algorithm},label={lst:matching}]
FUNCTION ExecuteSmartMatch(StudentProfile, ScholarshipDatabase):
    Initialize Array EligibleGrants = []

    // Step 1: Extract Student Attributes
    SET s_QPI = StudentProfile.QPI
    SET s_Income = StudentProfile.HouseholdIncome
    SET s_Degree = StudentProfile.DegreeProgram
    SET s_HasActiveGovGrant = StudentProfile.HasActiveGovernmentGrant
        // Boolean flag; TRUE if student holds a concurrent state-sponsored award

    // Step 2: Iterate through the internal scholarship tracks
    FOR EACH Grant IN ScholarshipDatabase:
        // Skip inactive or expired grants
        IF Grant.IsActive == FALSE OR Grant.Deadline < CURRENT_DATE THEN
            CONTINUE
        END IF

        // Step 3: Base Criteria Evaluation
        IF s_QPI >= Grant.MinimumQPI AND s_Income <= Grant.MaximumIncome THEN

            // Step 4: Degree Program Matching
            IF s_Degree IN Grant.EligibleDegrees OR Grant.EligibleDegrees == "ALL" THEN

                // Step 5: Policy Contradiction & Exclusion Resolution
                // Example: Resolving concurrent grant restrictions
                IF s_HasActiveGovGrant == TRUE AND Grant.AllowsMultipleGrants == FALSE THEN
                    CONTINUE   // Exclusion overrides eligibility; skip grant
                END IF

                // If all checks pass, append to user's dashboard
                EligibleGrants.APPEND(Grant)
            END IF
        END IF
    ENDFOR

    // Step 6: Compute fit-margin relevance score for secondary sorting
    FOR EACH Grant IN EligibleGrants:
        SET margin_QPI = s_QPI - Grant.MinimumQPI
            // Higher = stronger academic fit
        SET margin_Income = Grant.MaximumIncome - s_Income
            // Higher = stronger financial fit
        // Normalize and combine margins into a single relevance score.
        // Weights can be adjusted; equal weighting used here as a baseline.
        SET Grant.FitScore = (margin_QPI * 0.5) + (margin_Income / 10000 * 0.5)
    ENDFOR

    // Step 7: Return list sorted by deadline (primary), then by fit score (secondary)
    // Primary sort ensures time-critical grants appear first;
    // secondary sort differentiates between equally urgent grants by match strength.
    RETURN SORT(EligibleGrants,
                PRIMARY BY Grant.Deadline ASCENDING,
                SECONDARY BY Grant.FitScore DESCENDING)
ENDFUNCTION
\end{lstlisting}

\end{figure} Within that pipeline, the algorithm prioritizes the evaluation of hard constraints (QPI and household income) before moving to conditional constraints (degree program and exclusion flags). This structured flow ensures that the system efficiently filters out incompatible grants early in the process, minimizing database load and preventing mismatched applications from reaching the student's dashboard. Grants that pass all constraint checks are not returned on equal footing: the algorithm computes a fit-margin relevance score for each eligible grant, calculated from the degree to which the student's QPI exceeds the grant's minimum threshold and the degree to which the student's household income falls below the grant's maximum ceiling. The final output is sorted by deadline (ascending) as the primary key --- ensuring time-critical opportunities appear first --- and by fit score (descending) as the secondary key, so that among equally urgent grants, those where the student's profile most strongly satisfies the criteria are ranked higher. This differentiates ScholarPath AdDU from a binary pass/fail filter and provides students with a relevance-ordered, not merely deadline-ordered, scholarship dashboard.

\subsection{Dynamic Faceted Search and Backend Indexing Strategy}

To resolve the discovery friction caused by relying on basic keyword searches, ScholarPath AdDU employs a Dynamic Faceted Search architecture. This allows students to apply multiple intersecting filters (e.g., ``State-Sponsored Grants'' + ``Active Deadlines'' + ``Full Tuition Coverage'') to instantly narrow down the three available internal scholarship tracks without system latency. Backend Database Categorization: The database normalizes the scholarship data by strictly categorizing the three internal scholarship tracks into four primary metadata facets based on their funding origin: Internally Funded Endowments, Corporate \& External Foundations, State-Sponsored Grants, and Specialized Service Pipelines. (The exhaustive tabular dataset utilized to construct these database facets is detailed in Appendix A). Indexing Strategy for Real-Time Retrieval: To ensure real-time rendering without lag, the system's backend database (e.g., Supabase) utilizes Compound Indexing. Instead of scanning the entire database for every search query ($O(n)$ time complexity), the database pre-sorts records based on frequently queried combinations.

\begin{itemize}
  \item B-Tree Indexes are applied to exact-match categorical fields (e.g., Grant\_Category and Status).
  \item Range Indexes are applied to continuous numerical fields (e.g., QPI\_Requirement and Application\_Deadline).
  \item Client-Side Caching \& Debouncing: The frontend interface utilizes asynchronous JavaScript (AJAX/Fetch) combined with an input debounce of 300 milliseconds. This ensures that the database is only queried after the user finishes typing or selecting facets, drastically reducing server load and preventing UI stuttering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System Architecture and Cloud Infrastructure}

To resolve the operational bottlenecks caused by the physical submission of dense paperwork, such as Form 230-SCH and government-stamped tax returns, ScholarPath AdDU employs a secure, cloud-based architecture. The integration of the database, storage, and external communication services is mapped in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} below. \begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figure4_architecture.png}
  \caption{High-Level System Architecture of ScholarPath AdDU}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure} Technical Stack and Storage: The system is built on a three-layer technical stack selected for its cohesion and rapid prototyping capabilities. At the client layer, Vite serves as the frontend build tool, paired with a component-based JavaScript framework (React or Vue) to produce a fast, modular web interface. For cross-platform mobile deployment, Capacitor wraps this same Vite production build into native Android and iOS applications, eliminating the need to maintain a separate mobile codebase. At the backend layer, Supabase serves as the core Backend-as-a-Service (BaaS) infrastructure, providing a hosted PostgreSQL relational database for system state and metadata, Supabase Storage for large object files (i.e., the Document Vault), and Supabase Auth integrated natively with Row Level Security (RLS) policies. This unified ecosystem was selected for its seamless integration of database, storage, and security layers within a single managed service. Metadata Tagging and Virtual Attachment : To eliminate redundant file uploads, the Document Vault operates on a ``one-time upload, multi-attach'' architecture. When a student uploads a prerequisite document, the physical file is securely stored exactly once in a dedicated Supabase Storage bucket. The system generates a unique, secure file path for this object. Instead of duplicating the physical file for every new scholarship application, the PostgreSQL database utilizes metadata tagging. A Documents table records the file's metadata (e.g., StudentID, DocumentType, UploadTimestamp, and the Storage\_Path). When a student applies for multiple scholarships, the system simply creates a relational link (a foreign key) in the Applications table pointing back to the single original document record. This highly normalized architecture ensures optimized storage limits and rapid retrieval times for Scholarship Staff. Income Document Verification Workflow: The Document Vault's handling of proof-of-income documents follows a structured, administrator-validated verification process to ensure that scholarship applications are not processed on the basis of unreviewed financial data. When a student uploads a proof-of-income document --- such as a BIR-stamped Income Tax Return (ITR) --- to the Document Vault, the document is automatically assigned a verification status of Pending in the Documents table. A Scholarship Staff member subsequently reviews the uploaded document through the Unified Admin Dashboard, examining it against the requirements of the applicable scholarship. The staff member then manually updates the document's verification status to either Verified or Rejected based on their assessment. Scholarship applications linked to documents with a verification status of Pending or Rejected cannot proceed to interview or subcommittee evaluation. This workflow ensures that the eligibility matching engine operates on validated financial data while preserving the administrative authority of the University Scholarship Office over the authenticity determination --- a distinction that cannot be automated without integration with official government tax databases, which is outside the current system scope. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and Data Privacy Compliance: The platform processes highly sensitive economic and academic data, and its architecture is fundamentally governed by the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173). Encryption and Row Level Security (RLS): To ensure strict compliance and secure data handling, the system relies on Supabase Auth and PostgreSQL's native security features. All data in transit is secured using Transport Layer Security (TLS). Furthermore, access to sensitive data at rest --- both within the database tables and the Supabase Storage buckets --- is restricted using Row Level Security (RLS) policies. RLS ensures that access controls are enforced directly at the database level, preventing unauthorized queries regardless of how the frontend application is accessed. Rather than building separate portals for each administrative office, ScholarPath AdDU consolidates all evaluator functions into a single Unified Admin Dashboard. This design responds directly to the fragmented committee data identified in the problem statement: because each application passes sequentially through staff verification, interview, subcommittee evaluation, and executive endorsement (Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart}), office-specific portals would reintroduce the data silos and manual collation the system is meant to eliminate \cite{ref46,ref36}. A single interface writing to a single canonical record also ensures that every status change is attributable to an identified human actor within one audit log. Unification applies to the interface, not to access privileges: each user's view remains scoped by role through PostgreSQL Row Level Security (Table~\ref{tab:rbac}), preserving least-privilege access under RA 10173.


\subsection{Automated Notification Subsystem}

The absence of proactive alerts frequently results in missed application deadlines and subsequent student attrition. To rectify this, ScholarPath AdDU integrates an automated, multi-channel notification subsystem designed to deliver critical deadline alerts and status updates via SMS and email, without requiring manual administrative intervention. Third-Party API Integration: The system leverages enterprise-grade communication APIs to ensure reliable message delivery. The Resend API is utilized to format and dispatch detailed, formal email notifications regarding application outcomes and institutional updates. For time-sensitive alerts, such as approaching 48-hour deadlines, the system integrates the Iprogsms API to push brief, immediate text messages directly to the student's registered mobile device. Automated Trigger Logic and Edge Functions: To interface with the Supabase backend, the notification engine is driven by automated task schedulers (Cron jobs) and Supabase Edge Functions. The trigger logic evaluates two primary conditions: 1. Deadline Proximity: A scheduled backend worker queries the database daily. If the Current\_Date is within a predefined threshold (e.g., 3 days) of a Grant.Deadline AND the user has an application marked as Status = 'Draft', the system executes an Edge Function to send a ``Deadline Reminder'' payload via the Iprogsms API. 2. Status Mutation: Whenever an evaluator changes an application status through the Unified Admin Dashboard (e.g., from 'Pending' to 'Approved'), a database webhook immediately triggers an Edge Function. This function compiles the relevant context and pushes a ``Status Update'' payload to the Resend API, delivering the official results to the student's inbox.

\section{Testing \& Evaluation Procedures: ISO/IEC 25010 Task-Based Usability Protocol}

To assess the functional suitability and usability of the ScholarPath AdDU system, the study employs a mixed-method evaluation approach. This methodology combines task-based observational testing with a standardized System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire, administered to a purposive sample of approximately 10 to 15 end-users. This sample size ensures sufficient data saturation and will consist of scholarship applicants, University Scholarship Office staff, and selected School Subcommittee members, covering both the applicant portal and the Unified Admin Dashboard.

\subsection{Step-by-Step Testing Procedure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Briefing and Consent:} Participants are introduced to the system's purpose. The facilitator explicitly states that the system is being tested, not the user, and secures informed consent.
  \item \textbf{Task Execution:} Participants are asked to complete four specific, real-world scenarios designed to test core system features. The facilitator observes silently without offering assistance unless a critical failure occurs.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task 1: Profile Registration:} The user must create a new account, inputting required academic and socioeconomic data, such as QPI, household income, and degree program.
    \item \textbf{Task 2: Document Upload:} The user navigates to the Document Vault and successfully uploads a standardized test file (e.g., a mock Form 230-SCH), verifying that the system securely stores the file and registers the metadata appropriately.
    \item \textbf{Task 3: Dynamic Search and Application:} The user utilizes the dynamic faceted search to filter the available internal scholarship tracks to locate a specific ``Corporate \& External Foundation'' grant and successfully submit an application.
    \item \textbf{Task 4: Unified Admin Dashboard Navigation (Scholarship Staff participants only):} The user logs into the Unified Admin Dashboard, navigates to pending applications, and changes the status of a specific student application from ``Pending'' to ``Approved'' to trigger the automated notification subsystem.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Observational Data Collection:} During the execution of Tasks 1 through 4, the facilitator records specific usability metrics (detailed in Section 3.4.2).
  \item \textbf{Post-Session Instruments:} Immediately after completing the four tasks, participants are administered two sequential instruments in the following order. First, the participant completes the standardized 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire to assess overall subjective perceived usability of the ScholarPath AdDU interface. The SUS is administered first because it captures the participant's holistic impression of the system immediately after task completion, before any comparative framing is introduced. Second, the participant completes the Post-Prototype Perceived Effort Survey (Appendix C), which asks them to estimate the time and effort required by the digital process and to compare that perception against the manual experience they described in the pre-study. These are presented as two distinct instruments and are not to be conflated: the SUS measures general interface usability, while Appendix C generates the comparative data used to answer Research Questions 1, 2, and 3.
  \item \textbf{Debriefing:} A brief, semi-structured exit interview is conducted to gather qualitative feedback on any frustrations or difficulties encountered.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Data Analysis and Statistical Tools}

. To objectively quantify the system's performance and user satisfaction, the following metrics and statistical tools will be utilized:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Quantitative Observational Metrics:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Time-on-Task: Measured in seconds, this records the exact duration a user takes to successfully complete each of the four designated tasks.
    \item Error Rates: Calculated as the number of misclicks, failed uploads, or incorrect navigation paths taken per task, as well as the overall percentage of users who fail to complete a task independently.
    \item Notification Delivery Success Rate: Measured during Task 4 (Unified Admin Dashboard Navigation, Section 3.4.1), this metric records the percentage of triggered notification events --- specifically, application status mutations from 'Pending' to 'Approved' executed by the Scholarship Staff participant --- that result in a successfully dispatched notification to the student's registered contact channel (SMS via Iprogsms or email via Resend) within the testing session. The facilitator confirms delivery by verifying receipt on the designated student-side test device. The metric is computed as: (Number of Successfully Delivered Notifications / Total Triggered Notification Events) $\times$ 100. A delivery success rate of 100\% across the testing session is the target threshold, given the controlled, low-concurrency testing environment.
    \item Trigger-to-Delivery Latency: Recorded in seconds, this measures the elapsed time between the moment a Scholarship Staff participant commits a status mutation in the Unified Admin Dashboard (the event trigger) and the moment the corresponding notification is confirmed as received on the student-side test device (the delivery confirmation). The facilitator records both timestamps manually during Task 4. Mean latency across all triggered events in the session is computed and reported. This metric operationalizes the performance efficiency dimension of the notification subsystem as distinct from overall system response time.
  \end{itemize}
  \item System Usability Scale (SUS) Analysis:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The SUS provides a validated instrument for measuring participants' subjective perceived usability of the platform. Responses are gathered on a standard 5-point Likert scale across 10 items, with alternating positive and negative phrasing to reduce response bias. Raw SUS scores, ranging from 0 to 100, will be analyzed using descriptive statistics. The Mean will be calculated to determine the average perceived usability score across the purposive sample, providing an overall benchmark of system acceptability. To contextualize the resulting mean SUS score, the study will apply the adjective rating scale validated by Bangor et al. \cite{ref13}, which maps numerical ranges to descriptive quality grades: scores of 85 and above are classified as Excellent, 71 -- 84 as Good, 50 -- 70 as OK, and below 50 as indicative of usability failure requiring significant redesign. A target mean SUS score of 71 or higher --- the Good threshold --- is set as the minimum benchmark for the ScholarPath AdDU prototype to be considered functionally acceptable for its intended user base. The Standard Deviation will measure the variance in scores, indicating the degree of consensus among participants regarding the system's subjective usability. It is important to note that the SUS addresses Research Question 4 specifically --- overall system usability and performance efficiency --- and is analytically distinct from the pre/post comparative instruments described below, which address Research Questions 1 through 3.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Functional Correctness Evaluation (Smart Eligibility Checker Test Case Matrix)
  \begin{itemize}
    \item To directly assess whether the automated matching engine executes the Exclusion Flag Hierarchy without generating false positives or incorrect recommendations --- corresponding to the Functional Correctness sub-characteristic in Table~\ref{tab:iso25010} (ISO/IEC 25010) --- the study employs a structured test case matrix administered separately from the user-facing usability session. Fifteen representative synthetic student profiles are constructed to cover the full range of eligibility edge cases: (1) QPI exactly at the grant's minimum threshold; (2) QPI one decimal point below the minimum threshold; (3) household income exactly at the grant's maximum ceiling; (4) household income one unit above the ceiling; (5) degree program explicitly listed in a grant's eligible degrees; (6) degree program explicitly listed in a grant's exclusion list; (7) degree program absent from both eligible and excluded lists where the grant specifies ``ALL''; (8) student holding a concurrent active government grant applying to a grant where AllowsMultipleGrants = FALSE ; (9) student holding a concurrent active government grant applying to a grant where AllowsMultipleGrants = TRUE ; (10) student profile satisfying all base criteria but triggering a degree-specific exclusion flag; (11) an expired grant ( Deadline \textless{} CURRENT\_DATE ) that should be skipped regardless of eligibility; (12) a grant marked IsActive = FALSE ; (13) a student meeting no criteria for any grant (null result expected); (14) a student meeting criteria for multiple grants simultaneously, verifying correct multi-result output; and (15) a student whose profile satisfies both a broadly inclusive rule and a specific exclusion flag simultaneously, verifying that the exclusion unconditionally overrides the inclusion.
    \item For each profile, the expected system output --- either a specific set of matched grants or an empty result --- is documented prior to test execution. The actual system output is then recorded and compared against the expected output. A match constitutes a pass; any discrepancy constitutes a failure and is logged for root cause analysis. The Functional Correctness score is computed as: (Number of Passing Test Cases / Total Test Cases) $\times$ 100. A minimum pass rate of 100\% on the exclusion-flag test cases (profiles 6, 8, 10, 11, 12) is set as the non-negotiable threshold, as false positives on exclusion logic constitute the highest-risk failure mode of the matching engine.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Pre/Post Comparative Survey Analysis:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The Pre-Study Student Problem Validation Survey (Appendix B) and the Post-Prototype Perceived Effort Survey (Appendix C) together constitute the comparative data collection instruments for Research Questions 1, 2, and 3. Because both instruments use structurally parallel items with identical scale anchors, their data is analyzed through direct item-level mean comparisons using descriptive statistics.
    \item For Component 1 of both instruments (the ordinal time-estimation scale), the distribution of responses across the five ordinal ranges will be tabulated for both the pre- and post-prototype conditions. The modal response category and the direction of shift --- whether participants perceive the digital process as taking less, equal, or more time --- will be reported to address RQ1.
    \item For Component 2 of both instruments (the six-item perceived effort Likert grid), the Mean score per sub-task item will be calculated for both conditions and presented in a side-by-side comparison table. A decrease in mean score from pre- to post-prototype on a given sub-task indicates a perceived reduction in effort for that application stage. Sub-task items 1 and 3 through 5 inform RQ1; sub-task item 2 (eligibility criteria determination) informs RQ2; and sub-task item 6 (deadline tracking) informs RQ3.
    \item For Component 3 of the pre-study instrument and Component 3 of the post-prototype instrument, frequency distributions will be used for the categorical items concerning application mismatch history (RQ2) and deadline-miss history (RQ3). The proportion of participants who reported previously submitting ineligible applications will be compared against the proportion who indicated the Smart Eligibility Checker would prevent such errors. Similarly, the proportion who reported previously missing deadlines will be compared against post-prototype responses on the notification subsystem's perceived effectiveness.
    \item For Component 4 of the post-prototype instrument (the overall comparative perception item), a frequency distribution of responses across the five-point scale --- from Much Less Effort to Much More Effort --- will be reported as a summary indicator of the system's perceived overall impact, synthesizing the findings of RQ1 through RQ3.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Qualitative Data Analysis (Thematic Analysis): While the SUS provides a quantitative benchmark, the qualitative feedback gathered during the post-task semi-structured exit interviews will be processed using thematic analysis. The researchers will transcribe user comments and categorize them into recurring themes (e.g., ``Navigation Friction,'' ``Terminology Confusion,'' or ``Positive Interface Feedback''). Identifying these qualitative themes will provide contextual explanations for the quantitative error rates and guide the final iterative refinements of the system's user interface prior to deployment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-Study Student Problem Validation Survey and Post-Prototype Perceived Effort Survey}

To operationalize the within-subjects comparative design described in Section 3.1, this study employs two structurally paired survey instruments administered at distinct phases of the research process. The Pre-Study Student Problem Validation Survey (Appendix B) is administered prior to system exposure to establish baseline data on the perceived burden of the existing manual scholarship application process. The Post-Prototype Perceived Effort Survey (Appendix C) is administered immediately following the task-based usability testing session to capture participants' post-exposure perceptions using an identical scale structure. Together, these instruments generate the comparative data required to address Research Questions 1, 2, and 3, as the System Usability Scale administered in the same session addresses only RQ4. The paired design deliberately uses within-subjects administration --- the same participants complete both instruments --- to eliminate inter-group variability. Because each participant's pre-study responses serve as their own individual baseline, any observed reduction in perceived effort or time burden can be attributed to the system's features rather than differences in prior scholarship experience across participants. Both instruments use scaled and categorical response formats rather than open recall of specific durations, acknowledging that the manual application process unfolds over days or weeks and is not conducive to precise time measurement. Pre-Study Student Problem Validation Survey (Appendix B) The pre-study instrument is administered to a purposive sample of current or former AdDU scholarship applicants prior to prototype development or any system exposure. It is organized into three components, each targeting a distinct dimension of the manual process and a corresponding research question. Component 1: Perceived Time-on-Process asks respondents to estimate the total duration of their end-to-end manual application experience --- from initial scholarship discovery through physical document submission --- using five ordinal ranges: less than 1 day, 1 -- 3 days, 4 -- 7 days, 1 -- 2 weeks, and more than 2 weeks. This item establishes the time-burden baseline for RQ1. It should be noted, however, that post-prototype time estimates are derived from a controlled prototype testing session and may not accurately reflect the duration of the process in actual real-world deployment, where variables such as document readiness, network conditions, and platform familiarity over sustained use may produce different results. Component 2: Perceived Effort Scale applies a five-point Likert format (1 = Very Easy, 5 = Very Difficult) to six discrete application sub-tasks. The six sub-tasks are: (1) finding out which specific scholarships the student was eligible for; (2) determining whether they specifically met all eligibility criteria --- including QPI thresholds, household income ceilings, and degree program restrictions --- for a given scholarship prior to submitting; (3) gathering, printing, and preparing the required physical documents; (4) physically submitting the completed application package to the respective offices; (5) tracking the status of the application after submission; and (6) keeping track of multiple scholarship deadlines simultaneously throughout the application period. Sub-tasks 1, 3, 4, and 5 together establish the effort baseline for RQ1. Sub-task 2 specifically establishes the eligibility confusion baseline for RQ2. Sub-task 6 establishes the deadline management difficulty baseline for RQ3. Component 3: Application Accuracy and Deadline History uses categorical and multiple-choice items to establish behavioral frequency baselines for RQ2 and RQ3. Respondents are asked whether they have ever submitted an application and later discovered they did not meet the eligibility requirements for that scholarship, and if so, how many times this occurred. They are separately asked whether they have ever missed a scholarship application deadline under the manual process, and if so, what the primary reason was. These items produce the concrete behavioral baseline data against which the post-prototype instrument's feature-effectiveness responses are compared. Post-Prototype Perceived Effort Survey (Appendix C) The post-prototype instrument is administered to the same participants immediately following the completion of the task-based usability testing session and after the SUS questionnaire. Its structural mirroring of Appendix B is deliberate and essential: because both instruments use identical Likert anchors, ordinal ranges, and sub-task categories, their corresponding items can be directly compared without transformation. Component 1 presents the same five ordinal time-estimation ranges as the pre-study, asking participants to estimate how much total time the digital scholarship application process through ScholarPath AdDU would require. This mirrors the pre-study Component 1 for RQ1 comparison. Component 2 presents the same six perceived effort sub-tasks as the pre-study, reworded to reflect ScholarPath AdDU's specific features: scholarship discovery through the centralized repository; eligibility determination through the Smart Eligibility Checker; document management through the Document Vault; digital application submission; application status tracking through the student dashboard; and deadline awareness through the automated notification subsystem. The same five-point Likert anchors are used, allowing mean scores per sub-task to be compared directly against pre-study means. Component 3: System Feature Effectiveness moves beyond effort estimation to assess whether participants believe the system's specific features address the problems identified in the pre-study's Component 3. Participants indicate whether they believe the Smart Eligibility Checker would prevent them from applying to scholarships for which they do not qualify --- directly addressing the mismatch baseline from RQ2 --- and rate the extent to which the automated notification system would help them remain aware of upcoming deadlines --- directly addressing the missed-deadline baseline from RQ3. Component 4: Overall Comparative Perception asks participants to characterize the total effort required by ScholarPath AdDU relative to the traditional manual process, using a five-point scale ranging from Much Less Effort to Much More Effort. This item synthesizes the findings of RQ1 through RQ3 into a single directional indicator and provides a holistic perception anchor for the study's comparative conclusions. An optional open-ended item closes the instrument, inviting participants to identify which specific system feature most reduced the difficulty of the scholarship application process for them.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

Because the usability testing of ScholarPath AdDU involves human participants and the simulated input of sensitive socioeconomic data, the evaluation phase is strictly governed by established research ethics and the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173). The following ethical protocols will be implemented throughout the testing phase:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Informed Consent and Voluntary Participation: Prior to any system interaction, all participants will be provided with an informed consent form detailing the purpose of the study, the specific tasks required, and the estimated time commitment. Participation is strictly voluntary, and end-users maintain the right to withdraw from the testing session at any point without penalty or need for justification.
  \item Data Anonymization and Privacy: During the task-based execution (specifically Task 1: Profile Registration), participants will be explicitly instructed to use provided mock data or pseudonymized profiles rather than their actual household income or academic records. This ensures that no genuine sensitive information is exposed or recorded during the evaluation of the system's matching algorithm.
  \item Confidentiality and Secure Data Disposal: All observational metrics; pre-study and post-prototype survey responses (Appendices B and C), and SUS questionnaire responses will be kept strictly confidential. The qualitative and quantitative data will be aggregated so that no single metric or quote can be traced back to an individual participant. Upon the successful defense and final submission of the capstone project, all test accounts, mock databases, and physical consent forms utilized during the evaluation phase will be permanently and securely deleted to prevent any unauthorized data retention.
\end{itemize}
